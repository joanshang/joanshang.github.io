I'm Joan, a physician-scientist. Currently, I'm a Clinical Pathology resident at Harvard/Mass General Brigham. My work sits at the intersection of molecular pathology, immuno-oncology, and computational biology — and it's largely animated by one question: why do some patients respond to immunotherapy while others are harmed by it?

I completed my MD/PhD at the Icahn School of Medicine at Mount Sinai, where my doctoral work in the Faith Lab demonstrated that the gut microbiome confers susceptibility to immune checkpoint inhibitor–related colitis. That research, supported by an NIH F30 fellowship, produced a first-author paper in the Journal of Experimental Medicine on establishing that causation along with demonstrating the microbiome dynamics in immunotherapy-related colitis. Alongside it, I trained in computational genomics and machine learning in Dr. Ron Do's lab, building EHR-based models of disease risk.

Research interests

Immune-related adverse events. Checkpoint inhibitors have transformed oncology, but immune-related toxicity remains difficult to anticipate and difficult to manage. My dissertation work traced how gut microbial communities shift in patients who develop immunotherapy-related colitis, and I remain interested in the microbiome as both a biomarker and a modifiable variable in these syndromes.

Computational biology and clinical data. I build machine learning models on large-scale electronic health record data, most recently a model for coronary artery disease prediction (manuscript under review), and survival analyses examining how social determinants of health drive disparities in patient outcomes.


Training and path

I grew up in rural America, where good science felt distant rather than impossible. An early lecture on the microbiome — Dr. Bonnie Bassler's work, recommended to me by a mentor at Emory — was the moment the field stopped being abstract. Nine years in New York followed, doing exactly the kind of research I had once only read about.

Pathology found me through the clinic. Rotating through the wards, I kept following questions past the bedside and into the laboratory, and eventually realized that the laboratory was where I wanted to stand. Clinical pathology lets me hold both roles at once: the physician answering a specific patient's question, and the scientist asking why the answer looks the way it does.

I moved to Boston in 2026 to begin residency at Harvard/Mass General Brigham.
